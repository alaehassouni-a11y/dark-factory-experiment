# Bonsai soil and repotting

Special fertilizer is not necessary for a bonsai, but special soil is a wise investment, and regular repotting is what keeps a tree in a small pot alive.

## The soil

The best-known bonsai soil is akadama, from Japan. It is made of tiny grains of volcanic clay that hold moisture and nutrients while still draining well. Growers often combine it with other materials to make a custom mix; a common mixture is akadama, pumice and lava rock in the proportions one half, one quarter, one quarter. A mix with more akadama, or some compost, holds more water for someone who cannot water often.

Akadama is expensive and importing it is not especially environmentally friendly, so growers experiment with cheaper alternatives. Moler clay, a diatomaceous clay from Denmark, works in a similar way. Diatomaceous earth is often sold as cat litter; if you use it, choose one that is neither clumping nor scented.

Ordinary garden potting compost is not suitable on its own: a bonsai needs a substrate that drains well and offers the right balance of nutrients.

## Why repotting matters

A tree becomes pot-bound as it uses up the nutrients in its soil and its roots grow into the shape of the pot. With no room and no nutrients left, it stops growing, and if nothing is done it dies. Repotting, or transplanting, resupplies the tree with fresh soil and the nutrients it needs to grow. Most bonsai need repotting about every two years. A pot-bound tree is easy to recognise: when you water it, most of the water runs straight out because the compacted roots cannot absorb it.

## Roots

Roots are an essential part of a bonsai but easy to overlook because they are buried. The crown of the tree reflects what the root system does: many fine, branching roots produce a mature crown with a fine branch structure. Beginners should not work on the roots of a newly bought nursery plant right away; root pruning too early kills the tree outright. After a year or two of training the top of the plant, it will have a healthy, compact crown and enough spare root capacity for its first soil change and root reduction.

## The pot

The pot is a frame, not the picture. Choose one that harmonises with the look and feel of the tree, rather than one so large, gaudy or unusual that people look at the pot instead of the tree. A tree does not go into a bonsai pot until its trunk has reached the size and shape you want, which takes years; the largest and best trees are not potted for twenty years or more.

A kokedama, or moss ball, made of compost and sphagnum moss, is a sustainable alternative to a pot: it can hang or sit in a dish.

## Giving a tired tree a rest

A tree that has been weakened, by a pest outbreak or a few missed waterings on a hot day, can be moved for a year or two into a temporary container a little bigger than its pot: a plastic crate, a polystyrene box or even a flower bed. Once it is stronger and healthier, it will withstand being put back into its old pot. A change is as good as a rest for an unhappy bonsai.
