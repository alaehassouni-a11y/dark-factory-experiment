# Getting started with bonsai

Most people meet bonsai by buying a cheap, ready-made tree. It is the easiest way in, but it is far more interesting, cheaper and more rewarding to grow and style your own. Bonsai is not about owning trees; it is the care and the training that make a bonsai, and the enjoyment is in creating one.

## Do not buy a bonsai first

A real bonsai takes at least five years of development to be convincing and costs several hundred dollars. The cheap trees sold in shops and supermarkets, a two-year-old juniper cutting dropped into a bonsai pot, are not bonsai: they have had none of the care and training. Buying one is a poor way to begin and usually ends in a dead tree.

Instead, read as much as you can, look at as many bonsai as you can, even in pictures, and work out what you like about them. Until you can see a bonsai in your mind you cannot create one. Then buy an inexpensive nursery plant in a one-gallon pot that looks interesting, ideally a shrubby one, and start training it. Learning the basics takes about two years; styling is learned over a lifetime.

## The first year

Do not repot the plant and do not touch its roots. Beginners are always impatient to move a tree into a bonsai pot, but a tree only goes into one when it is basically finished, after years of training. Working on the roots right away kills the plant. Work on the top instead: style it by pruning, remove no more than about a third of the foliage in one session with a few months between sessions, and it will thrive and become compact, looking more like a bonsai and less like a bush. It may die anyway; that is part of learning.

If you keep the plant alive in its nursery container for a year, it will be healthy and compact with spare root capacity, and ready for its first soil change and root reduction. Join a club if you can; repotting is best learned by watching it done, or doing it with guidance.

## Indoors or outdoors

Everyone wants a bonsai on the coffee table, but bonsai is basically an outdoor activity. Even in cold countries people grow outdoor bonsai, protected in winter, and that is easier than growing them indoors. Indoor bonsai is difficult and limited to tropical species. If indoors is the only option, choose a ficus: cheap, easy to find, tolerant of a lot of abuse, and available with a decent trunk at a reasonable price. Choose a species suited to your climate, ideally one native to your area, and decide where the tree will live before you get it.

## Other ways to get a tree

A ready-made bonsai from a specialist shop comes in many species, shapes and sizes, at a high price for the older ones. Pre-bonsai or nursery stock is a more economical route to quick results. Collecting a tree from the wild is cheaper still but tricky, and needs the landowner's permission. Growing from seed or cuttings is the cheapest and slowest way: a young tree takes around three to five years before it can be styled, so it suits a side project alongside a tree you can style sooner.

## Keep it in perspective

It is easy to get wrapped up in the art, the tools, the special soils and feeds. Now and then step back: you are looking after a pot plant. And enjoy the tree at whatever stage it is on, rather than waiting for it to be finished; when a bonsai is finished, it is dead. As the saying goes, the best time to plant a tree was ten years ago, and the second-best time is today.
